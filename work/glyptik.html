Case study

Glyptik, master's thesis research, 2024-2025

What makes people trust an AI meeting summary?

I designed three versions of the same AI meeting recap and watched 15 professionals decide which one to believe. The answer changed how I design AI interfaces.

Role
Researcher and product designer, solo
Context
MA thesis, Innovation Design Management, UE Potsdam
Method
3 Figma prototypes, 15 think-aloud sessions and interviews
Constraint
Same meeting content in every version, so only the design varied
Outcome
10 of 15 chose the version that shows its sources. A framework and a product plan followed

The problem

People use AI meeting summaries every day. Many still don't trust them, so they reread the transcript and lose the time the tool was supposed to save.

Research on trust in AI mostly covers explainability and fairness. It says very little about the interface itself: which icon, label or sentence makes an AI output feel dependable. Designers fill that gap with intuition.

I wanted evidence. My thesis looked at semiotics, the study of signs and how people read them, and asked whether the signs in an AI interface change how much people trust it.

Three hypotheses

H1

Visual signs like icons, symbols and colour cues raise trust.

H2

Language, like tone, metaphor and phrasing, makes the tool easier to use.

H3

Visual and verbal cues together beat either one alone.

Setting up a fair test

I framed every session around one realistic moment: you missed a team meeting, and an AI has summarised it for you. That situation forces people to rely on the AI, so trust shows up in what they do, not just in what they say.

The first decision was what to compare. A single polished design would only tell me whether people liked it. Instead I built three versions of Glyptik's summary screen with identical content: a 30-minute product launch meeting with three decisions and two tasks. Only the signs changed, so any difference in trust came from the design.

Fifteen professionals took part, aged 22 to 35, from UX and product design, product management, software engineering and architecture. Each 30 to 45 minute remote session combined a think-aloud walkthrough of all three versions with a semi-structured interview. I coded the transcripts with thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke).

What 15 people chose

Version C won clearly. Participants trusted it most because they could check it, not because it told them to. 13 of 15 valued the timestamps or source links.

Four themes came out of the analysis: trust depends on transparency, people balance simplicity against detail, they want to validate claims themselves, and confidence labels confuse more than they reassure.

The core idea held: the signs in an interface change trust. But the most trusted signs were evidence, like timestamps, rather than decoration, like icons.

Three design calls the evidence settled

Decision 1

Show sources, not scores

Link every claim to the moment it was said. A confidence score asks people to trust a number they can't inspect.

RejectedA confidence badge as the main trust signal

13 of 15 valued timestamps or source links. The “AI Confidence: High” badge split the room: 6 found it useful, 6 were confused or put off, and 3 didn't notice it. Uncertainty belongs on specific claims, with a reason, not on the whole summary.

Decision 2

Disclose AI without sounding the alarm

Label AI content where it appears, and pair the caveat with a way to verify it.

RejectedA large “may contain errors” banner above the summary

6 of 15 found the large disclaimer off-putting, and only 3 valued it. Even people who chose Version C wanted it toned down. Disclosure is necessary. The way it's framed decides whether it builds trust or erodes it.

Decision 3

Layer the detail, don't remove it

Start clean and let people open the transcript and sources when they want them.

RejectedMinimalism as the default signal of credibility

Only one participant preferred the minimal version. Most found it too thin to trust. But preferences split on density: some wanted the transcript open, others wanted it behind a toggle. The answer is user control, not one fixed level of detail.

The framework

I turned the findings into a semiotics-based framework for designing trustworthy AI interfaces. It has four parts.

Semiotic transparency

Make it obvious what the AI produced and how reliable it is, with clear, familiar labels.

Contextual validation

Link every AI output to its evidence, like a timestamp or transcript line, so people can check it.

Symbolic clarity and balance

Use intuitive symbols, and balance simplicity against enough context to decide.

Adaptive user control

Let people choose the level of detail: summary or full transcript, collapsed or expanded.

In the thesis I applied it to a second scenario, an AI-driven team performance dashboard, to show it holds beyond meeting summaries.

From one screen to a product plan

The thesis tested a single screen. A real product has to earn trust before, during and after the summary.

So I went back to all 15 transcripts and synthesised what the rest of the app needed: personas, a journey map, an information architecture, key flows and an app-wide trust layer. Every claim in the plan points to the participants it came from.

The principle

Show, don't vouch.

The AI earns trust by pointing to evidence, naming what it did, and asking before it acts for other people.

Three ways people catch up

6 of 15

Verifiers

High stakes, sceptical. Want the transcript open and every source one click away.

4 of 15

Skimmers

Time-poor, higher baseline trust. Want a short layered summary and to copy it out.

3 to 4 of 15

Coordinators

Run team work. Want owners, due dates and tasks that reach their own tools.

An MVP that closes the trust loop

Sources and quiet disclosure, claim-level uncertainty, the Tasks hub, copy and export, a shared-link preview, Home, and minimum consent and privacy settings. Ask AI waits until it can cite a source on every answer.

Honest about the evidence

Every step of the journey is tagged observed, said or assumed. The organiser persona and the consent flow rest on signals only, so they come first in the research plan.

Reflection

What this study can't tell you

  • Fifteen English-speaking, tech-savvy professionals. Less digitally fluent users, and other languages and cultures, may read these signs differently.
  • It captured first impressions. Trust builds and breaks over weeks of real use.
  • People tested prototypes, not a live AI that makes real mistakes.

What I'd do next

  • Interview 5 to 6 meeting hosts to validate the organiser side and the consent flow, the weakest evidence in the plan.
  • Run a 2-week diary study with the coded prototype on real meetings, to see whether people verify less as trust is earned.
  • Test claim-level uncertainty against the global badge, measuring comprehension, not just preference.
